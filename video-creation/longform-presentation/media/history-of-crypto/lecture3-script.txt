The History of Crypto (1996 – Today)
Lecture Script — Speaker Notes
How to use this script: Each section corresponds to one slide. The bullet points on the slide are your cue — this document contains everything you say around them. Speak naturally and in your own words where possible.
SLIDE 1 — Introduction
WHAT TO SAY
Welcome to Lecture 3. We've now established what money is and what Bitcoin is. In this lecture we're going to zoom out and look at the full arc of history — from the very first ideas around digital currency in the late 1980s and 1990s, all the way through to where we are today.
This is important because crypto didn't appear out of nowhere in 2009. It was the culmination of decades of ideas, experiments, failures, and breakthroughs. Understanding that history gives you context that most people in this space simply don't have — and it makes you a much more informed participant.
Let's start at the beginning.
💡 This lecture covers a lot of ground — keep each section moving at a good pace. The goal is context, not a history textbook.
SLIDE 2 — The Cypherpunk Movement
WHAT TO SAY
Most people think Bitcoin started in 2009. But the ideas behind it were being discussed twenty years earlier, in a community called the Cypherpunks.
The Cypherpunks were a loose group of activists, mathematicians, engineers, and computer scientists who shared a core belief: that privacy is a fundamental right, and that cryptography — the science of secure communication — was the tool that could protect that right in the digital age.
They communicated through the Cypherpunk Mailing List, an email group that started in the early 1990s and became the intellectual birthplace of many ideas that eventually led to Bitcoin. Some of the most important thinking in the history of digital finance happened in those email threads.
A few key figures stand out. Timothy C. May wrote the Crypto Anarchist Manifesto in 1988, which laid out the vision for a future where cryptography would allow people to transact anonymously and beyond government control. Eric Hughes wrote A Cypherpunk's Manifesto in 1993, which opened with the now-famous line: 'Privacy is necessary for an open society in the electronic age.'
And then there's David Chaum. Chaum is arguably the grandfather of digital cash. As far back as 1989, he founded a company called DigiCash and created the first real attempt at anonymous digital currency. He was decades ahead of his time.
The core belief running through all of this was simple: governments and corporations cannot be trusted with your financial data. People needed tools to protect their own financial privacy. That belief is the philosophical foundation on which Bitcoin was eventually built.
💡 The Cypherpunk movement is often overlooked but it's genuinely fascinating. If you want to go deeper, Eric Hughes' Cypherpunk Manifesto is a short read and still feels remarkably relevant today.
SLIDE 3 — Failed Attempts at Digital Money
WHAT TO SAY
Before Bitcoin, there were several serious attempts to create digital money. They all failed — but each one taught the world something important, and their failures directly shaped what Bitcoin became.
DigiCash, founded by David Chaum in 1989, was the first real attempt. It was actually technically impressive for its time — it used cryptographic protocols to allow anonymous transactions. But it required banks to participate as intermediaries, it was centrally controlled, and it never gained critical mass. DigiCash filed for bankruptcy in 1998.
Then there was e-gold, which launched in 1996. E-gold was digital currency backed by actual gold held in vaults. At its peak it had millions of users and processed billions of dollars in transactions. But it was centralized — run by a single company — and the US government eventually prosecuted its founders for running an unlicensed money transmission business. It was shut down in 2008.
Two proposals from 1998 are particularly interesting because they came closest to what Bitcoin eventually became. Nick Szabo — a computer scientist and legal scholar who many people suspect may actually be Satoshi Nakamoto — proposed Bit Gold. It had many of Bitcoin's core features: a proof-of-work system, a decentralized ledger, and a capped supply. But it was never actually implemented.
Around the same time, a developer named Wei Dai published a proposal called b-money, which also described a decentralized digital currency. Satoshi Nakamoto actually cited Wei Dai's work in the Bitcoin whitepaper.
So what do all of these have in common? They either relied on a central authority that could be shut down, or they failed to solve what's called the double-spend problem — the challenge of preventing someone from spending the same digital coin twice. Without solving that problem, you can't have trustworthy digital money. Bitcoin was the first system to solve it — without requiring any central party.
💡 The double-spend problem is worth pausing on briefly. It's the core technical challenge that all of these attempts couldn't crack. Bitcoin's breakthrough was solving it through the blockchain and proof-of-work — which is something we'll cover in depth in the technology section.
SLIDE 4 — Satoshi Nakamoto & The Bitcoin Whitepaper
WHAT TO SAY
Now we get to the moment that changed everything.
On October 31st, 2008 — Halloween — a person or group going by the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine-page document to a cryptography mailing list. The title was: 'Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System.' That document is what we now call the Bitcoin whitepaper, and it is one of the most important technical documents ever written.
In it, Satoshi laid out a complete, working solution to the double-spend problem — without any central authority. It described the blockchain, the proof-of-work consensus mechanism, and the entire architecture of what Bitcoin would become. It was not a vague concept or a proposal. It was a blueprint. And it worked.
Who is Satoshi Nakamoto? Nobody knows. To this day, the true identity behind that name has never been confirmed. Multiple people have been suggested over the years — Nick Szabo, Hal Finney, Craig Wright has infamously claimed to be Satoshi — but none of it has ever been conclusively proven. Satoshi deliberately kept their identity hidden, and it remains one of the greatest mysteries in the history of technology.
On January 3rd, 2009, Satoshi mined the very first Bitcoin block — known as the Genesis Block. And embedded directly into that block was a message: 'Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks.' That was a headline from The Times newspaper published that exact day. It was not an accident. It was a statement. Bitcoin was born as a deliberate response to the broken financial system.
Nine days later, on January 12th, Satoshi sent 10 Bitcoin to a computer scientist named Hal Finney — the first ever Bitcoin transaction between two people. Hal Finney was a Cypherpunk, one of the earliest Bitcoin adopters, and by many accounts one of the people most likely to have contributed to Bitcoin's early development.
Then in 2010, something remarkable happened. Satoshi began handing off control of the Bitcoin codebase to other developers, gradually withdrew from the project, and eventually disappeared entirely. No more posts, no more emails. Gone. And crucially, the roughly one million Bitcoin that Satoshi is believed to have mined in the early days have never been moved. To this day.
💡 The Satoshi mystery genuinely captivates people. Don't rush through it. The combination of anonymity, the Genesis Block message, and the unmoved Bitcoin holdings makes for a compelling story.
SLIDE 5 — The Early Days of Bitcoin (2009–2012)
WHAT TO SAY
In the very early days, Bitcoin was essentially worthless in monetary terms. It was a technical experiment being run by a small community of developers, cryptographers, and libertarian-minded enthusiasts who believed in its potential — but there was no market for it, no exchange rate, no way to buy anything with it.
That changed on May 22nd, 2010, in what is now one of the most famous events in crypto history. A programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz posted on the Bitcoin Talk forum offering to pay 10,000 Bitcoin to anyone who would order him two pizzas. Someone took him up on it. Two Papa John's pizzas were purchased for 10,000 Bitcoin — and that became the first documented commercial transaction using Bitcoin. We now celebrate May 22nd every year as Bitcoin Pizza Day.
Those 10,000 Bitcoin, at Bitcoin's all-time high, would have been worth over $700 million. Laszlo has said publicly he doesn't regret it — he was happy to help establish that Bitcoin had real-world value. And he was right. That transaction proved something important: Bitcoin could be used as money.
Also in 2010, a website called Mt. Gox launched as Bitcoin's first major exchange. Mt. Gox — which originally stood for Magic: The Gathering Online Exchange, because that's what the site was before it pivoted to Bitcoin — would go on to handle around 70% of all Bitcoin transactions at its peak. And it would later become the site of one of crypto's biggest disasters, which we'll get to shortly.
Through 2011 and into 2012, the ecosystem slowly grew. Developers started building on top of Bitcoin. Libertarians and political activists adopted it as a tool for financial freedom. Early adopters began accumulating. And in February 2011, Bitcoin hit $1 for the first time — a milestone that felt significant to the community even if the rest of the world wasn't paying attention yet.
💡 Bitcoin Pizza Day is always a crowd-pleaser. It makes the early days feel tangible and real. It also sets up a great discussion about how early adoption works and why being early in any new technology can be so valuable.
SLIDE 6 — The Wild West Era (2012–2016)
WHAT TO SAY
This era is called the Wild West for good reason. It was a period of extraordinary growth, genuine innovation, and also some of the darkest chapters in crypto's history.
Let's start with the dark side. In 2011, a darknet marketplace called Silk Road launched on the Tor network. Silk Road allowed people to buy and sell illegal goods — primarily drugs — using Bitcoin, because of its pseudonymous nature. At its peak it was doing hundreds of millions of dollars in transactions. The FBI shut it down in 2013 and arrested its founder, Ross Ulbricht. Silk Road handed crypto's critics their strongest argument, and the 'Bitcoin is for criminals' narrative that we tackled in Lecture 2 is largely rooted in this period.
Then came the Mt. Gox collapse. In February 2014, Mt. Gox — at the time handling the vast majority of global Bitcoin trading — announced it had lost approximately 850,000 Bitcoin due to what it claimed was a long-running hack. At the time that was worth around $450 million. Mt. Gox filed for bankruptcy, its CEO was arrested, and hundreds of thousands of users lost their funds. It was a catastrophic event for crypto's credibility, and Bitcoin's price collapsed. The mainstream media declared Bitcoin dead — which, as you'll see, became something of a recurring theme.
But here's where the breakthrough comes in. In 2015, a young programmer named Vitalik Buterin launched Ethereum. Ethereum took the core idea of Bitcoin — a decentralized blockchain — and extended it with something called smart contracts. A smart contract is a piece of code that lives on the blockchain and executes automatically when certain conditions are met. No intermediary needed. No lawyer, no bank, no middleman.
This was a watershed moment. Ethereum opened the door to programmable money — the idea that you could build entire financial systems, applications, and digital economies on top of a blockchain. Everything that came after — DeFi, NFTs, DAOs, token ecosystems — traces its roots back to that moment in 2015.
Also during this period, Bitcoin's halving mechanism began to play out in earnest. Every four years, the reward given to Bitcoin miners is cut in half — reducing the rate at which new Bitcoin enters circulation. This supply shock has historically preceded major price increases, and it's one of the key drivers of Bitcoin's four-year market cycle. We'll cover halving in much more detail in the trading and fundamentals sections.
💡 The Mt. Gox story is important because it led directly to the 'not your keys, not your coins' principle that the crypto community now lives by. It's worth planting that seed here even if you cover it in depth in the security section later.
SLIDE 7 — The First Three Bull Runs
WHAT TO SAY
One of the most important things to understand about Bitcoin is that it moves in cycles. And those cycles follow a remarkably consistent pattern. Let's walk through the first three.
Cycle one. Bitcoin's price climbed from essentially zero to around $32 in June 2011. It had gained attention from tech enthusiasts and libertarians, there had been a wave of media coverage, and speculation drove the price up rapidly. Then it crashed. From $32, Bitcoin fell around 93% — dropping all the way back down to around $2. The community declared it dead. It wasn't.
Cycle two. Over the next two years, Bitcoin recovered and then some. By December 2013, it had reached approximately $1,150 — a new all-time high. This time the catalyst included growing mainstream awareness, increasing merchant adoption, and significant interest from China. Then it crashed again — this time around 85% — driven partly by the Mt. Gox collapse we just discussed. Again, the headlines said Bitcoin was finished. Again, they were wrong.
Cycle three. This is the one most people remember. By late 2017, Bitcoin had climbed to nearly $20,000. This cycle coincided with the ICO — Initial Coin Offering — mania. Thousands of new tokens and projects launched almost overnight, many of them with little more than a whitepaper and a promise. Billions of dollars poured into the space. It felt like the whole world was suddenly talking about crypto. And then, once again, it crashed — this time around 84%, with Bitcoin falling all the way back to around $3,200 by the end of 2018.
Now step back and look at the pattern across these three cycles. Each peak was dramatically higher than the last. Each crash wiped out most of the gains — but not all of them. After each crash, the price bottomed out at a level significantly higher than where it had been before the previous cycle began. Higher highs. Higher lows. Longer timeframes. This is the pattern that Bitcoin traders and long-term holders have come to recognize and respect.
💡 This is one of the most practically valuable slides in the course. The cycle pattern directly informs trading strategy and long-term investing decisions. Make sure this lands clearly — it will resonate throughout the rest of the course.
SLIDE 8 — Maturation & Institutional Adoption (2019–2021)
WHAT TO SAY
After the brutal bear market of 2018, the industry spent 2019 rebuilding. New projects were being built, infrastructure was being developed, and a more mature, professional ecosystem was starting to take shape. Then 2020 arrived, and things exploded.
The summer of 2020 saw the rise of what became known as DeFi — Decentralized Finance. For the first time, people could lend, borrow, trade, and earn yield on their crypto assets without using any bank or centralized institution. All of it ran on smart contracts, primarily on Ethereum. Billions of dollars poured into DeFi protocols almost overnight. It was chaotic, risky, and genuinely revolutionary.
At around the same time, NFTs — Non-Fungible Tokens — began to enter the mainstream conversation. Digital artists, musicians, and creators started using blockchain technology to sell unique digital assets. In March 2021, an artist called Beeple sold a digital artwork as an NFT at Christie's auction house for $69 million. The world took notice.
But perhaps the most significant shift in this period was the arrival of institutional money. MicroStrategy, a publicly traded business intelligence company, began converting its corporate treasury into Bitcoin in August 2020 — a decision its CEO Michael Saylor made public and defended loudly. Tesla followed, announcing a $1.5 billion Bitcoin purchase in early 2021. Square, PayPal, and a wave of other major companies began integrating Bitcoin into their businesses.
In June 2021, El Salvador made history by becoming the first country in the world to adopt Bitcoin as legal tender — meaning it must be accepted as payment for goods and services across the country. Whatever you think of the policy, it was a landmark moment for Bitcoin's legitimacy on the world stage.
All of this drove Bitcoin to a new all-time high of approximately $69,000 in November 2021. The previous cycle's peak of $20,000 — which had seemed unimaginable just a few years earlier — had been left far behind. The pattern had repeated again.
💡 The institutional adoption story is important because it fundamentally changed the narrative around Bitcoin. It was no longer just a tool for tech enthusiasts and libertarians — it was now on the balance sheets of publicly traded companies. That shift matters.
SLIDE 9 — The Crypto Winter & Beyond (2022–Today)
WHAT TO SAY
And then 2022 happened. What followed the November 2021 peak was one of the most destructive periods in crypto's history — not just in terms of price, but in terms of fraud, negligence, and lost trust.
It started in May 2022 with the collapse of Terra and its associated token LUNA. Terra was a so-called algorithmic stablecoin — a token designed to maintain a $1 value through a complex mechanism involving LUNA. When that mechanism broke down under selling pressure, it entered a death spiral. Within a matter of days, approximately $40 billion in value was wiped out. People lost their life savings. It was a catastrophic failure that sent shockwaves through the entire industry.
The contagion spread. Three Arrows Capital, one of the most prominent crypto hedge funds in the world, had massive exposure to the Terra collapse and imploded. Celsius, a crypto lending platform that had marketed itself aggressively to retail investors as a safe place to earn yield, froze withdrawals and eventually filed for bankruptcy. Billions of dollars belonging to ordinary people were locked up.
And then came FTX. In November 2022, FTX — at the time the world's second-largest crypto exchange, run by Sam Bankman-Fried — collapsed in spectacular fashion. It emerged that customer funds had been secretly used to make risky bets through Bankman-Fried's trading firm, Alameda Research. It was fraud on a massive scale. Sam Bankman-Fried was arrested, tried, and convicted. Bitcoin dropped below $16,000. Once again the media declared crypto dead.
Now — this is where those industry standard definitions on the slide become important. In the crypto trading community, we use specific technical benchmarks to define market conditions rather than just relying on gut feel or media narratives.
A crypto winter is defined as Bitcoin's price being at or below the 200-week simple moving average. The 200-week SMA is a long-term trend indicator — when price drops to that level, it signals that the market has given back years of gains and is in true long-term distress. Historically, these periods have represented the best long-term buying opportunities in Bitcoin's history.
A bear market, more broadly, is defined as Bitcoin trading below its 50-week SMA and/or the 200-day SMA. These are the moving averages that traders watch most closely to determine the medium-term trend. When price is below them, conditions are bearish. When it reclaims them, conditions are improving. We'll go much deeper into moving averages and how to use them in the technical analysis section of this course.
The recovery from the 2022 lows was driven by several factors — rebuilding confidence, continued development across the ecosystem, and a landmark regulatory moment. In January 2024, the US Securities and Exchange Commission approved Bitcoin spot ETFs for the first time. This was a massive deal — it meant that ordinary investors could gain exposure to Bitcoin through traditional brokerage accounts, opening the door to trillions of dollars in potential capital. Bitcoin surged on the news and went on to set new all-time highs in 2024.
And that brings us to today. The crypto industry is older, more mature, more regulated, and more integrated into the traditional financial system than it has ever been. The early Wild West days are behind us. That doesn't mean the risks are gone — they're not. But the foundation is stronger than it's ever been, and the opportunity set is larger than it's ever been.
In the next lecture, we look at how crypto compares to the traditional financial system. See you there.
💡 This is a powerful closing to a dense lecture. The 2022 events are still raw for many people in the community — acknowledge that. And end with genuine optimism about where things stand today. The arc from cypherpunks in the 90s to Bitcoin ETFs approved by the SEC is a remarkable story.
