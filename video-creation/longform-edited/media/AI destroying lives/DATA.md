**Plain-language summary the sources support (for the strategist, not a quote):** ask people whether AI will cut jobs and about seven in ten say yes. Ask the ones with a job whether they worry about their own and about three in ten say yes. Two separate polls found that in the same week of March 2026, and Pew found the same shape in 2015, 2017 and 2022. Blue-collar workers show it most: nearly three quarters expect AI to cut jobs, and three in five say they are not concerned at all about theirs. The gap is closing slowly: worry about one's own job rose from 21 to 30 percent in a year at Quinnipiac, and from 15 to 18 at Gallup. The honest line is not "people are fools". It is that almost everyone expects this to happen to someone else.

#### C3. Why people underestimate the risk to their own job (one study, opened)

- **Lergetporer, Wedel, Werner, "Automatability of Occupations, Workers' Labor-Market Expectations, and Willingness to Train", IZA Discussion Paper No. 16687, December 2023** (Technical University Munich and ifo Institute). https://docs.iza.org/dp16687.pdf (read 2026-11-10).
- Abstract, verbatim: "In our representative online survey, respondents on average underestimate the automation risk of their occupation, especially those in high-automatability occupations."
- "A majority of 67.5 percent of respondents underestimate their automatability, while 21.4 percent overestimate it. Only 11.1 percent are within the accuracy range".
- Workers in the control group "believe that 26.8 percent of core tasks in their current jobs are automatable", "significantly below the actual average automatability for their occupations (52.1 percent)."
- The gap is widest where the risk is highest. Medians: "approximately 5 percentage points for respondents with low automatability (20.0 vs. 25.0 percent) while it is large at 45 percentage points for respondents with high automatability (30.0 vs. 75.0 percent)." And: "beliefs about their own jobs' automatability are largely flat across the whole spectrum of actual automatability."
- The authors tie it to a known pattern: "favorable misperceptions in other labor-market beliefs, for example, optimistic bias in job seekers' beliefs about their job-finding probability".
- **Cautions (all must travel with the number):** Germany, not the US. Surveyed "in May/June 2022", before ChatGPT. "The overall sample size is 3,012 respondents", all employed. "Automatability" here means the share of an occupation's core tasks a computer or computer-controlled machine could do with technology as of 2019, per the German employment agency's research institute. It is NOT the chance of losing the job. A discussion paper, which IZA itself calls "preliminary work"; whether a journal version exists was not checked.
- Airable shape: "In a German study, workers in the most automatable jobs guessed that about 30 percent of their work could be done by a machine. The measured figure was 75."
- Nothing else is listed. The classic optimism-bias papers were not opened, so they are skipped rather than stretched.
