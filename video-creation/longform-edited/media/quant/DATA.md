### Pillar 2: The story of why it exists (the mechanism, in Quant's own words, for the whiteboard spine)

- **The problem Quant says it saw first.** Verdian on camera (Oracle AI World Tour session, audio-verified, see CLIP-3): "people were building and designing blockchain and DLT without standardization. Everyone was building things in isolation. Everyone was creating another chain that does the same thing as every other chain. And the problem that I saw very early on was around 2013 ... we're building things that can't connect or interoperate with each other." Quant's 2018 release: "The inability of blockchains to work together is a widely recognised issue for blockchain technology."
- **Walled gardens of bank money (the 2026 version of the problem).** Quant's TCH post, verbatim: "Today, tokenised deposits mostly live in walled gardens, with a bank's tokenised money typically moving only within its own system. The result is fragmentation: multiple chains, multiple networks, and multiple pools of value that can't talk to each other. This is where Quant comes in. We enable interoperability and programmability so that tokenised deposits issued by one institution can move freely across the banking system." (https://quant.network/news/quant-powers-the-clearing-houses-on-chain-money-initiative-in-the-us/, 2026-09-24, read 2026-11-04).
- **What a tokenised deposit is (TCH's definition, airable verbatim).** "Tokenized deposits are digital representations of a financial institution deposits, which retain the protections and regulatory oversight of a traditional deposit, but are recorded and moved differently. Tokenized deposits move automatically within rules institutions set in advance. For businesses, this means liquidity and payments processed around the clock. For financial institutions, it means joining a shared network rather than building one alone." (TCH release 2026-09-24). UK Finance's: "Tokenised deposits are digital representations of traditional commercial bank money" (GBTD page).
- **The lock, credit, settle mechanism (a London-to-Singapore payment, Quant's own worked example, perfect for WB scenes).** "Lock. The London bank locks the manufacturer's funds the moment it accepts the instruction. ... Credit. The Singapore bank credits the supplier straight away against that lock. ... Settle. The two banks settle with each other in the next available window, and the lock is released. No new asset is introduced, and nothing leaves the banking system. At every step, the money is a deposit at a regulated bank." (https://quant.network/perspectives/programmable-cross-border-payments-what-they-are-and-how-to-build-the-use-case/, Alex Chiriac, 2026-10-27, read 2026-11-04).
- **Synchronised settlement (all legs or none).** "Payment infrastructure has traditionally settled each leg of a transaction independently. That works until two or more movements need to succeed or fail as a single event, at which point sequencing itself becomes the risk: one leg clears, the other hasn't, and there's a window where the transaction sits in limbo"; RT2 lets funds be "earmarked and reserved, then released only once agreed terms are satisfied across every party involved, in effect, a prepare-and-commit model" (https://quant.network/perspectives/inside-the-bank-of-englands-synchronisation-lab-treasury-rebalancing-use-case/, Alex Chiriac, 2026-07-30, read 2026-11-04). The treasury version: "Large corporates and upper-SMEs typically operate across multiple domestic banks. Treasury teams rebalance liquidity using sequences of independent CHAPS or high-value payments, executed one leg at a time ... Using Quant Flow, treasury actions such as liquidity rebalancing, sweeps, or internal funding would be packaged into a single synchronised settlement bundle. Each participating bank prepares and reserves funds before settlement, with all legs committed together or not at all." (Synchronisation Lab release, 2026-02-13).
- **The remortgage (the UK retail story, real and live).** GBTD page: "Two remortgage completions, where deposit funds were locked and released automatically at completion. Customers could also keep earning interest until completion." and "One consumer marketplace transaction, where a buyer's funds were locked and released only when a private sale's goods were successfully exchanged." Lloyds' Peter Left at Sibos 2026: Lloyds will give corporate clients wallets "mainly as an interface for running programmable workflows, such as the conveyancing use case built with Quant that automates the drawdown and repayment of funds in a remortgage" (https://quant.network/events/from-pilots-to-production-five-takeaways-from-sibos-2026/, 2026-10-08, read 2026-11-04).
- **Why banks want it (programmability is the feature).** Reena Verma, Head of Tokenised Deposit Commercialisation at The Clearing House, quoted by Quant: "Our banks chose one feature, which has been echoed by my panellists, which is programmability." The On-Chain Money Initiative's MVP "focuses on cross-border payments with multiple conditions and parties, and treasury payments between entities" (same Sibos 2026 post).
- **Plain-language summary the sources support (for the strategist, not a quote):** every bank keeps its own ledger and its own pool of money; those pools cannot talk to each other, and the new tokenised versions mostly cannot either. Quant does not ask the bank to move to a new chain; it sits on top of what the bank already runs, connects it to the other ledgers through one API layer, and lets the money carry rules: lock it here, release it only when the other side has delivered, settle every leg at once or not at all. The bank keeps custody, the regulator keeps oversight, and the money never leaves the banking system.
