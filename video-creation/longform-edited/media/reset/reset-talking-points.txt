Slide 2 · Can't Pay It. Can't Cancel It.
On screen: 10.6T comes due within 12 months (1 of every 3 dollars) · Old debt 3.5% vs new 10-year 5.0% · Owed inside the US: around 77% · The Fed, Social Security, banks, pensions, insurers, savers · China 1.5%.
Talking points
    • Around 10.6 trillion comes due within a year, around one third of everything the government owes in the bond market. It gets paid off the usual way: with new loans.
    • Old debt costs around 3.5 percent on average. A new 10-year loan costs around 5. So every year cheap old debt gets swapped for expensive new debt, and the interest bill keeps climbing.
    • So why not just cancel it? Because around three quarters of it is owed inside the US: the Fed, the Social Security trust funds, banks, pensions, insurers, regular savers. China? Around 1.5 percent.
    • "Cancel this debt, and we'd be cancelling it on ourselves."

Slide 3 · The Playbook   (CH3)
On screen: 1946: 106% of GDP / 1974: 22% · Bonds paid 2.5% / Prices rose 19.7% · Today: 98% (FY2025).
Talking points
    • 1946, right after World War II: the debt held by the public was 106 percent of the whole economy. Still the record.
    • Starting in 1942 the Fed capped long-term government bond rates at 2.5 percent. Then prices took off: in March 1947, inflation hit 19.7 percent. So bondholders earned 2.5 a year while prices went up almost 20.
    • Growth helped too. But by 1974 that debt was down to around 22 percent of the economy.
    • And today that same measure is around 98 percent. Close to the record again.

Slide 4 · It Ran Again in 2020
On screen: Rates at zero (Mar 2020 to Mar 2022) · Inflation 9.1% (June 2022) · Total debt 133% to 116% of GDP · Reinhart and Sbrancia quote · Top 10%: 88% of stocks, bottom 50%: 0.6%.
Talking points
    • March 2020, the Fed cut rates to zero, and they stayed there for 2 years. Then inflation hit 9.1 percent in June 2022. (Say it in that order: zero for 2 years, THEN 9.1. Not "at the same time".)
    • Total debt measured against the economy fell from around 133 percent to around 116 percent by early 2023.
    • Economists call it financial repression: the interest you get paid stays below inflation, so every year the debt is worth a little less in real money. And so are your savings.
    • Reinhart and Sbrancia (BIS): it works best "when accompanied by a steady dose of inflation."
    • Rewatch line (the video's only one): "If that was a bit complex, feel free to push the J key on your computer to go back and watch that again."
    • Who pays? Savers, people on a paycheck, anyone holding bonds. Who wins? Whoever owns the assets. The top 10 percent own around 88 percent of stocks and funds, the bottom half 0.6 percent.
    • "Inflation doesn't hit everyone the same. It moves wealth from the people who save, to the people who own."
